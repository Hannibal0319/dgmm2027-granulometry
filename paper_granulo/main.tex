\begin{abstract}
The usual way to measure size distributions is to open an image by the digital disks of increasing radius that common libraries provide. We show that this is not a granulometry: these disks violate Matheron's absorption axiom for most pairs of radii, and the resulting size distributions can increase with the radius. Two remedies exist. The first is classical: replacing each opening by the union of openings by all larger disks yields a correct granulometry that is as round as the disks themselves and costs the same. We recommend it in place of single openings. The second keeps a \emph{single} structuring element per size, as in octagons, periodic lines or Bresenham decompositions, and it forces polygonal shapes. Our main result proves that this is unavoidable. If the mean radius of a Minkowski chain grows by at most $\delta$ per element, every element of radius $\rho$ deviates from every disk by at least about $\rho/(16\pi^2\delta^2)$, in any dimension. An explicit chain of periodic lines attains this rate within a factor of 4, and for a fixed library the exact slope is attained by octagons. Experiments on synthetic and real images confirm the diagnosis, the remedy, and when the anisotropy of single-element families matters.
\keywords{Granulometry \and Digital disk \and Minkowski sum \and Lattice polygon \and Width function \and Pattern spectrum}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Granulometries, introduced by Matheron~\cite{matheron1975}, formalise sieving: a family of openings $(\gamma_\lambda)_{\lambda\ge0}$ that are decreasing in $\lambda$ and satisfy the absorption law $\gamma_\lambda\gamma_\mu=\gamma_\mu\gamma_\lambda=\gamma_{\max(\lambda,\mu)}$. The size distribution and pattern spectrum derived from them~\cite{maragos1989,serra1982,soille2003} are among the most widely used morphological descriptors. The textbook construction uses a \emph{single structuring element per size}: $\gamma_\lambda(X)=X\circ B_\lambda$. Absorption then holds if and only if $B_\mu$ is $B_\lambda$-open, i.e.\ a union of translates of $B_\lambda$, for all $\lambda\le\mu$. Euclidean disks satisfy this condition.

On the grid $\Z^2$ they do not. The digital disks $\{p\in\Z^2:\|p\|\le r\}$ of common software are not open with respect to each other (Sect.~\ref{sec:violation}): with \texttt{scikit-image}~\cite{vanderwalt2014}, $73\%$ of the pairs of radii up to $40$ violate the condition. Size distributions computed as $r\mapsto|X\circ D_r|$, the most direct use of these libraries, then increase with the radius on most random test images. There are two ways out.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Several structuring elements per size.} The sup-closure $\gamma'_r=\bigvee_{s\ge r}\gamma_{D_s}$, i.e.\ the union of the openings by all disks of radius at least $r$, is a granulometry for any family $(D_s)$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:sup}). With digital disks it is correct, as round as the disks, and computable with one opening per radius. The construction is a classical consequence of Matheron's theory~\cite{matheron1975,serra1982}, and opening transforms~\cite{vincent1994} and local thickness~\cite{hildebrand1997} implicitly rely on it. We do not claim it as new. Our point is that it, rather than single disk openings, should be used to compute size distributions with disks.
\item \textbf{One structuring element per size.} The classical alternative builds the elements as \emph{Minkowski chains} $D_k=D_{k-1}\oplus C_k$, which are automatically granulometric~\cite{adams1993,jones1996,soille2003,vincent1994}. Examples are octagons from neighbourhood sequences~\cite{rosenfeld1968,farkas2006}, periodic lines~\cite{jones1996} and Bresenham segments~\cite{adams1993}. These families are polygonal. We prove that this is unavoidable and determine the price.
\end{itemize}
Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Diagnosis} (Sect.~\ref{sec:violation}). We quantify the violation of the axiom by standard digital disks and its effect on size distributions.
\item \textbf{Lower bound for single-element chains} (Theorem~\ref{thm:lower}, Corollaries~\ref{cor:mean}--\ref{cor:library}). If the mean radius of a Minkowski chain grows by at most $\delta$ per element, then \emph{every} element of radius $\rho$ has error $\varepsilon\ge(1-o(1))\,\rho/(16\pi^2\delta^2)$, where the error is the Hausdorff distance to the nearest Euclidean disk. If the fitted radius grows by bounded steps, the maximal error is at least $c\,\rho^{1/3}$. For a fixed library the slope $\kappa/(1+\kappa)$ is given by a linear program, and octagons attain it.
\item \textbf{Matching upper bound and higher dimensions} (Theorems~\ref{thm:upper} and~\ref{thm:zn}). An explicit chain of periodic lines achieves $\Theta(\rho/\delta^2)$, within a factor of 4 asymptotically, and $\Theta(\sqrt\rho)$ when $\delta\sim\rho^{1/4}$. In $\Z^n$, $\varepsilon\ge c_n\rho\,\delta^{-2n}$.
\item \textbf{Evidence and recommendations} (Sect.~\ref{sec:experiments}). On synthetic and real images, we compare single disk openings, their sup-closure and single-element families, on whole size distributions and on per-object sieve sizes. The comparison confirms that the classical sup-closure should replace single openings. When a single element per size is required, our LP-weighted periodic-line chains halve the error of octagons; this is a niche, and they do not compete with the sup-closure.
\end{itemize}
In short, round and correct disk granulometries on $\Z^2$ exist, but they need several structuring elements per size. Our contribution is to show that the common practice fails, to explain why the single-element route cannot be repaired, and to state what to use instead; we do not propose a new granulometry. Related work: chamfer norms~\cite{borgefors1986,thiel2001} approximate Euclidean balls by polygons with fixed edge directions, and Corollary~\ref{cor:library} applies when their balls form Minkowski chains (e.g.\ $3\times3$ masks). Neighbourhood sequences~\cite{farkas2006} optimise the roundness of a fixed library. Soille and Talbot~\cite{soille2001} noted that radial decompositions by Bresenham lines are not granulometric. Normand~\cite{normand2003} builds convex structuring elements by unions of translates, which lies outside the scope of our bound. To our knowledge, no lower bound of this kind has been published.

\section{Preliminaries}\label{sec:prelim}
For a finite set $X\subset\Z^n$ and a unit vector $u$, let $h_X(u)=\max_{p\in X}\langle p,u\rangle$ be the support function and $w_X(u)=h_X(u)+h_X(-u)$ the \emph{width function}, i.e.\ the width of $\conv X$ in direction $u$. Both are additive under Minkowski addition~\cite{schneider2014}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:additive}
h_{X\oplus Y}=h_X+h_Y,\qquad w_{X\oplus Y}=w_X+w_Y .
\end{equation}
The \emph{anisotropy} of $X$ is $\A(X)=\max_uw_X-\min_uw_X$, and its \emph{mean radius} is $\bar r(X)=\frac12\int w_X\,d\sigma$, where $\sigma$ is the uniform probability measure on the sphere; $\bar r$ is additive as well. In the plane, Cauchy's formula gives a concrete meaning: $\bar r(X)$ is the perimeter of $\conv X$ divided by $2\pi$, i.e.\ the radius of the circle with the same perimeter. For a segment of length $L$ it equals $L/\pi$. We say that $X$ is \emph{$\varepsilon$-round with radius $\rho$} if $\dH(\conv X,B(c,\rho))\le\varepsilon$ for some centre $c$. Then $|h_X(u)-\langle c,u\rangle-\rho|\le\varepsilon$, and hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:round}
2\rho-2\varepsilon\le w_X\le2\rho+2\varepsilon,\qquad \A(X)\le4\varepsilon,\qquad|\bar r(X)-\rho|\le\varepsilon .
\end{equation}
The opening of $Y$ by a structuring element $D$ is $Y\circ D=\bigcup\{D+t: D+t\subseteq Y\}$. A family $(D_k)$ generates a \emph{single-element granulometry} $\gamma_k(Y)=Y\circ D_k$ if and only if $D_l\circ D_k=D_l$ for all $k\le l$~\cite{matheron1975,serra1982}; since the relation is transitive, consecutive elements suffice. A \emph{Minkowski chain} is a family with $D_k=D_{k-1}\oplus C_k$, and every Minkowski chain generates a single-element granulometry. Its \emph{increments} are the $C_k$, its \emph{steps} are $\bar r(D_k)-\bar r(D_{k-1})=\bar r(C_k)$, and, if all $C_k$ belong to a finite set $\mathcal L$ (up to translation), $\mathcal L$ is its \emph{library}. Without the single-element restriction, any family of structuring elements can be turned into a granulometry.

The following fact is classical~\cite{matheron1975,serra1982}; we include a short proof for completeness.

\begin{proposition}[sup-closure]\label{prop:sup}
For any finite sets $D_1,\dots,D_R\subset\Z^n$, the operators $\gamma'_r=\bigvee_{s=r}^R\gamma_{D_s}$, $r=1,\dots,R$, where $\gamma_{D}(Y)=Y\circ D$, form a granulometry.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
A supremum of openings is an opening~\cite{serra1982}, and $\gamma'_r$ is decreasing in $r$. Let $r\le s$. Since $\gamma'_r$ and $\gamma'_s$ are anti-extensive and increasing, $\gamma'_r\gamma'_s\le\gamma'_s$ and $\gamma'_s\gamma'_r\le\gamma'_s$. Conversely, for every $t\ge s$ we have $\gamma'_r\gamma'_s\ge\gamma_{D_t}\gamma_{D_t}=\gamma_{D_t}$ and $\gamma'_s\gamma'_r\ge\gamma_{D_t}\gamma_{D_t}=\gamma_{D_t}$, and taking the supremum over $t\ge s$ gives $\ge\gamma'_s$.\qed
\end{proof}

Computing $\gamma'_r$ from $r=R$ down to $1$ needs one opening per radius, the same cost as the single-element family $\gamma_{D_r}$. The structuring elements are unchanged, so with digital disks the sup-closure is exactly as round as the disks. The rest of the paper explains why the single-element route cannot achieve this.

\section{Lower Bounds for Single-Element Chains}\label{sec:lower}
Additivity~\eqref{eq:additive} turns the roundness of two elements of a chain into a constraint on the increments between them. All edges of the increments are lattice vectors. If the increments are short, these vectors leave a gap of directions around each axis (Fig.~\ref{fig:idea}b), and a polygon whose edges avoid an angular interval cannot be round (Fig.~\ref{fig:idea}c). Adding more elements to the chain makes the polygon larger but does not fill the gap, so the deviation grows with the radius.

\begin{lemma}[direction gap]\label{lem:gap}
Let $\ell\ge1$. No nonzero $(a,b)\in\Z^2$ with $a^2+b^2\le \ell^2$ has a direction angle in $(0,\arctan(1/\ell))$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
A direction in that interval requires $a>0$ and $b\ge1$, hence $b/a\ge1/a\ge1/\ell$.\qed
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:lower}
Let $(D_k)_{k=0}^K$ be a Minkowski chain in $\Z^2$ whose increments have diameter at most $\ell\ge1$. If $D_0$ is $\varepsilon_0$-round with radius $\rho_0$ and $D_K$ is $\varepsilon_K$-round with radius $\rho_K$, then, with $c=1-\cos\bigl(\frac12\arctan\frac1\ell\bigr)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bound}
4(\varepsilon_0+\varepsilon_K)\ \ge\ 2\,(\rho_K-\rho_0-\varepsilon_0-\varepsilon_K)\,c .
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Step 1.} Every edge of $\conv C_k$ joins two lattice points, so it is a lattice vector of norm at most $\ell$.
\emph{Step 2.} Let $P=\sum_k\conv C_k$ and $Q=\frac12(P-P)$. By \eqref{eq:additive}, $W:=w_{D_K}-w_{D_0}=w_P=2h_Q$. The edges of the centrally symmetric polygon $Q$ are parallel to edges of the $\conv C_k$, so by Lemma~\ref{lem:gap} (applied to $\pm e$) none has a direction in $(0,\alpha)$, where $\alpha=\arctan(1/\ell)$. The normal angles in $(\frac\pi2,\frac\pi2+\alpha)$ therefore belong to the normal cone of a single vertex $q$ of $Q$: $h_Q(\theta)=|q|\cos(\theta-\theta_q)$ on an interval $I$ of length at least $\alpha$.
\emph{Step 3.} We may assume $s:=\rho_K-\rho_0-\varepsilon_0-\varepsilon_K>0$, since otherwise \eqref{eq:bound} is trivial. By \eqref{eq:round}, $\min W\ge2s$, so $h_Q>0$ and $|\theta-\theta_q|<\frac\pi2$ on $I$. On an interval of length $\alpha$ within $(\theta_q-\frac\pi2,\theta_q+\frac\pi2)$, the function $\cos(\theta-\theta_q)$ varies by at least $1-\cos(\alpha/2)$: by at least that much if $\theta_q\in I$, and by at least $1-\cos\alpha$ otherwise, by monotonicity. With $|q|\ge\min h_Q\ge s$ this gives $\max W-\min W\ge2sc$.
\emph{Step 4.} Finally $\max W-\min W\le\A(D_K)+\A(D_0)\le4\varepsilon_K+4\varepsilon_0$ by \eqref{eq:round}.\qed
\end{proof}

Only the first and the last element enter the bound. For a chain starting from a single point ($\varepsilon_0=\rho_0=0$), \eqref{eq:bound} gives $\varepsilon_K\ge\rho_K\,c/(2+c)$, where $c=\frac{1}{8\ell^2}(1+O(\ell^{-2}))$: \emph{every} element is at least $(1-o(1))\,\rho/(16\ell^2)$ away from a disk. Different constraints on the steps bound $\ell$ in different ways.

\begin{corollary}[mean-radius steps]\label{cor:mean}
If a Minkowski chain starting from a point has mean-radius steps $\bar r(C_k)\le\delta$, then $\ell\le\pi\delta$, and every element of radius $\rho$ has error $\varepsilon\ge\rho\,c/(2+c)=(1-o(1))\,\rho/(16\pi^2\delta^2)$, with $c=1-\cos(\frac12\arctan\frac1{\pi\delta})$. In particular, granulometric chains with bounded steps have error growing linearly with the radius.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
By Cauchy's formula the perimeter of a planar convex set is $\pi$ times its mean width, and it is at least twice its diameter. Hence $\diam\conv C_k\le\frac\pi2\cdot2\bar r(C_k)\le\pi\delta$.\qed
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[fitted-radius steps]\label{cor:fitted}
If all elements of a Minkowski chain are $\varepsilon$-round with radii satisfying $\rho_k-\rho_{k-1}\le\Delta$, then $\varepsilon\ge(1-o(1))\,((\rho_K-\rho_0)/512)^{1/3}$ as $\rho_K\to\infty$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
By \eqref{eq:additive} and \eqref{eq:round}, $w_{C_k}=w_{D_k}-w_{D_{k-1}}\le2\Delta+4\varepsilon$, so $\ell\le2\Delta+4\varepsilon$. If $\varepsilon$ stayed bounded, then so would $\ell$, and \eqref{eq:bound} would force $\varepsilon\to\infty$. Hence $\ell=4\varepsilon(1+o(1))$, and \eqref{eq:bound} with $\varepsilon_0=\varepsilon_K=\varepsilon$ becomes $8\varepsilon\ge(\rho_K-\rho_0)/(64\varepsilon^2)\,(1+o(1))$.\qed
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[fixed library]\label{cor:library}
Let $\mathcal L$ be a finite library containing a set with more than one point, and let
\[\kappa(\mathcal L)=\inf\Bigl\{\tfrac14\A\bigl(\textstyle\sum_C\lambda_Cw_C\bigr):\lambda\ge0,\ \min_u\textstyle\sum_C\lambda_Cw_C(u)=2\Bigr\}.\]
Then $\kappa:=\kappa(\mathcal L)>0$, and the last element of every chain with library $\mathcal L$ satisfies $\varepsilon_K\ge\bigl(\kappa(\rho_K-\rho_0-\varepsilon_0)-\frac14\A(D_0)\bigr)/(1+\kappa)$, i.e.\ $\varepsilon\ge\frac{\kappa}{1+\kappa}\rho-O(1)$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
For $\lambda$ in the simplex, $f(\lambda)=\A(\sum\lambda_Cw_C)/\min\sum\lambda_Cw_C$ takes values in $(0,\infty]$: $\sum\lambda_Cw_C$ is the width function of the polygon $\sum\lambda_C\conv C$, which does not have constant width, because bodies of constant width are strictly convex~\cite{schneider2014}. Moreover $f$ is continuous where finite and tends to $\infty$ where the minimum vanishes, so it attains a positive minimum, and $\kappa=\frac12\min f>0$. For the chain, write $W=w_{D_K}-w_{D_0}=\sum_Cn_Cw_C$ and $s=\frac12\min W\ge\rho_K-\rho_0-\varepsilon_0-\varepsilon_K$. Then $\A(W)\ge4\kappa s$ and $\A(W)\le4\varepsilon_K+\A(D_0)$.\qed
\end{proof}



We compute $\kappa$ by linear programming on a grid of 3600 directions, with all $D_4$-images included. It is $0.2071$ for $\{N_4\}$ or $\{N_8\}$ (squares) and $0.0412$ for octagons. For libraries of the 8-connected digital straight segments (DSS) of all primitive $v$ with $\|v\|\le m$, it is $0.0176$ for $m=\sqrt5$ and $0.0028$ for $m=10$. For the corresponding periodic lines $\{0,v\}$ it is $0.0137$ and, for $m=5$, $0.0038$. Since the grid underestimates the anisotropy, the computed values are lower approximations, and the bounds remain valid. For example, $\kappa(\{N_4\})=(\sqrt2-1)/2$ is the anisotropy of the square. Corollary~\ref{cor:library} is tight for octagons: the element of fitted radius $961.5$ of the best octagon chain has $\varepsilon/\rho=0.039571$, while $\kappa/(1+\kappa)=0.039570$.

\section{Matching Upper Bound and Higher Dimensions}\label{sec:upper}
The lower bound is attained, up to constants, by a simple explicit chain. For $m\ge1$, let $V_m$ contain one vector from each pair $\pm v$ of integer points on the boundary of $[-m,m]^2$. These are $4m$ vectors of norm at most $\sqrt2m$, and consecutive directions differ by at most $\arctan(1/m)\le1/m$, the largest gap being between $(m,0)$ and $(m,1)$. Let $Z$ be the polygon circumscribed to the unit disk whose edges have the directions of $\pm V_m$; it is centrally symmetric, hence $Z=\sum_{v\in V_m}[-\frac{\ell_v}2\hat v,\frac{\ell_v}2\hat v]$ with $\hat v=v/\|v\|$, and $1\le h_Z\le\sec(\frac1{2m})$. For $t\ge0$ set $n_v(t)=\lfloor t\ell_v/\|v\|\rfloor$ and
\[
D(t)=\bigoplus_{v\in V_m}n_v(t)\{0,v\},
\]
the Minkowski sum of $n_v(t)$ copies of the periodic line $\{0,v\}$~\cite{jones1996}. As $t$ grows, the counts increase. When several counts increase at the same $t$, we add the corresponding lines one at a time, so the elements form a Minkowski chain of periodic lines starting from $\{0\}$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:upper}
This chain has mean-radius steps at most $\sqrt2m/\pi$, and every element is $\varepsilon$-round with some radius $\rho$, with $\varepsilon\le\rho/(8m^2)+2m^2$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
A step adds one periodic line, whose mean radius is $\|v\|/\pi\le\sqrt2m/\pi$. Every element, including those created when ties are broken, satisfies $n_v=t\ell_v/\|v\|-\varphi_v$ for some $t$ and $\varphi_v\in[0,1]$. The centred support function of $\conv D=\sum_vn_v[0,v]$ is therefore $H=\sum_vn_v|\langle v,u\rangle|/2=t\,h_Z-\sum_v\varphi_v|\langle v,u\rangle|/2$. Since $\sum_v\|v\|\le4\sqrt2m^2$ and $\sec y-1\le y^2$ for $y\le\frac12$, we get $t-2\sqrt2m^2\le H\le t+t/(4m^2)$. The disk of radius $\rho=(\max H+\min H)/2\ge t-2\sqrt2m^2$ is thus within $\varepsilon\le t/(8m^2)+\sqrt2m^2\le\rho/(8m^2)+\sqrt2m^2+\frac{\sqrt2}4\le\rho/(8m^2)+2m^2$.\qed
\end{proof}

With $\delta=\sqrt2m/\pi$, Theorem~\ref{thm:upper} gives $\varepsilon\le\rho/(4\pi^2\delta^2)+\pi^2\delta^2$, while Corollary~\ref{cor:mean} gives $\varepsilon\ge(1-o(1))\,\rho/(16\pi^2\delta^2)$. For $\rho\ge4\pi^4\delta^4$, where the constant term is dominated, the best achievable error is therefore $\Theta(\rho/\delta^2)$, with constants within a factor of $8$, and of $4$ asymptotically. We can also concatenate chains with $m_j=2^j$, each run up to $t_j\propto16^j$ on top of the frozen previous ones; anisotropies and radii add. The result is a single chain with steps $O(\rho^{1/4})$ and error $O(\sqrt\rho)$, which is optimal for steps $O(\rho^{1/4})$ by Corollary~\ref{cor:mean}. Numerically (Fig.~\ref{fig:error}b), the fixed-$m$ chains have slope about $1/(18m^2)$, between the bounds $1/(32m^2)$ and $1/(8m^2)$, and the multiscale chain satisfies $\varepsilon\le0.28\sqrt\rho$ for $20\le\rho\le5\cdot10^4$.

Like all our bounds, Theorem~\ref{thm:upper} concerns convex hulls. Sums of periodic lines can leave isolated holes, as in~\cite{jones1996}. Adding the $2\times2$ square reduces them to $0.2$--$2\%$ of the hull's lattice points, and holes do not affect the granulometric property.

\paragraph{Higher dimensions.} In $\Z^n$ the directions are not ordered, but a measure argument replaces Lemma~\ref{lem:gap}. We state the result with unspecified constants; its exponent is not sharp, since for $n=2$ it gives $\ell^{-4}$ instead of $\ell^{-2}$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:zn}
Let $(D_k)$ be a Minkowski chain in $\Z^n$, $n\ge2$, whose increments have diameter at most $\ell\ge1$, and let $D_0$ and $D_K$ be $\varepsilon_0$- and $\varepsilon_K$-round (with respect to Euclidean balls). There are constants $b_n>0$ such that \eqref{eq:bound} holds with $c$ replaced by $1-\cos\alpha_n$, where $\sin\alpha_n=1/(4b_n(2\ell+1)^n)$. Hence $\varepsilon_K\ge c_n(\rho_K-\rho_0-\varepsilon_0)\ell^{-2n}-\varepsilon_0$. If the mean-radius steps are at most $\delta$, then $\ell\le2\delta/\mu_n$ with $\mu_n=\Gamma(\frac n2)/(\sqrt\pi\,\Gamma(\frac{n+1}2))$, and bounded steps again force linear growth.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[sketch]
The directions $u$ split into regions in each of which a single vertex of $Q=\frac12(P-P)$ attains $h_Q(u)$ (the normal fan of $Q$). The boundaries between these regions lie in the hyperplanes $e^\perp$ for the edges $e$ of the $\conv C_k$, i.e.\ lattice vectors with $\|e\|\le \ell$. There are at most $(2\ell+1)^n$ of them, and each band $\{u:|\langle u,e/\|e\|\rangle|<s\}$ of the sphere has measure at most $b_ns$. So some $u_0$ satisfies $|\langle u_0,e\rangle|\ge2\sin\alpha_n\|e\|$ for all $e$, and the geodesic cap of radius $\alpha_n$ around $u_0$ meets no boundary. On this cap $h_Q(u)=\langle q,u\rangle$ is linear. Restricting it to the great circle through $u_0$ and $q$ gives a variation of at least $|q|(1-\cos\alpha_n)$, and Steps 3 and 4 are as before. Finally, a segment of length $L$ has mean width $\mu_nL$, and mean width is monotone under inclusion.\qed
\end{proof}

\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiments}
\paragraph{Families.} We compare Gauss disks (\texttt{disk}), their sup-closure (Proposition~\ref{prop:sup}), and five single-element families with mean-radius steps between $1.2$ and $2.2$, i.e.\ about one element per integer radius. These are octagons; Bresenham/DSS segments as in~\cite{adams1993}; periodic lines as in~\cite{jones1996} in 8 and in 13 directions (the $D_4$-images of $(1,0),(1,1),(2,1)$ and, for 13, also $(3,1)$); and a chain of individual DSS computed by mixed-integer programming (HiGHS solver), which minimises the maximal error for $r\le48$. Segment libraries use the optimal weights $\lambda^\ast$ of Corollary~\ref{cor:library}. We move towards the multiplicities $\operatorname{round}(t\lambda^\ast)$ one set at a time, greedily keeping the element roundest. These chains contain the $2\times2$ square, and all their elements are hole-free.

Among single-element families, the optimised chains halve the error of octagons and reach a third of that of Bresenham decompositions, but they remain three times less round than the disks used by the sup-closure. With real multiplicities, a linear program finds monotone DSS families with error at most $0.43$ up to radius $2000$ (numerically; not proven), which points to integrality, used in Step~1 of the proof, as the obstruction.

\paragraph{Size distributions under rotation.} For each scene $X$, rotated by every multiple of $5^\circ$ up to $45^\circ$, we compute $F_\theta(r)=|\gamma_r(X_\theta)|/|X_\theta|$, implementing openings by chain elements as cascades of erosions and dilations by their elementary sets. We report the rotation sensitivity $S=\operatorname{mean}_r\operatorname{std}_\theta F_\theta(r)$ and the number of increases $F_\theta(r+1)>F_\theta(r)$, which are impossible for a granulometry. The scenes are nine squares of sides 17 to 81 pixels, rasterised exactly after rotation ($r\le40$), and three real images from \texttt{scikit-image}: fluorescence microscopy of nuclei (\texttt{human\_mitosis}, $r\le15$), \texttt{coins} ($r\le30$), and a material texture (\texttt{gravel}, $r\le20$). The real images are smoothed, rotated with bilinear interpolation, and thresholded at the Otsu level of the unrotated image.

\paragraph{Per-object sieve size.} Sieving assigns to each object $O$ the size $s(O)=\max\{r:\gamma_r(X)\cap O\ne\emptyset\}$ of the largest element that fits inside it, which is the same for Gauss disks and their sup-closure. \paragraph{Results.} Table~\ref{tab:rotation} and Fig.~\ref{fig:rotation} confirm the picture from the theory.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{The classical sup-closure is the best choice, as expected.} On every scene it has exactly the rotation sensitivity of Gauss disks, it never increases, and it removes the 17 increases that single Gauss-disk openings produce on the synthetic scene; on the random images of Sect.~\ref{sec:violation}, it removes all increases (0 of the 400 images, against 253 of 400 for single openings).
\item \emph{Single-element families pay for their polygonal shape.} On the synthetic scene, octagons are 7.5 times more orientation dependent than the disks, and the 13-direction periodic chain reduces this to 2.4 times.
\item \emph{The cost shows on large objects.} Per object, on coins (inscribed radii 18--30 pixels), octagons measure sizes with 4.8\% error and 1.1\% rotation scatter, against 2.8\% and 0.65\% for the disks. The 13-direction chain has the smallest scatter (0.54\%) and a 4.1\% error.
\item \emph{It does not show on small objects or on whole images.} On nuclei and gravel, with inscribed radii of 3--10 pixels, discretisation dominates: every family errs by 13--24\% and none is consistently best. On whole size distributions of the real images, all families agree within the effect of interpolation and thresholding.
\end{itemize}
The anisotropy of single-element families matters most for large or polygonal objects. The failure of single disk openings is a correctness issue on any image.

\paragraph{Recommendations.}
\begin{itemize}
\item To compute size distributions or pattern spectra with disks, do not open by each library disk separately. Use the sup-closure of Proposition~\ref{prop:sup}: open by every radius from the largest down and accumulate the unions. This costs nothing extra and yields a correct and round granulometry.
\item Only if a single structuring element per size is required, e.g.\ to implement each opening as a fixed cascade of two-point erosions and dilations whose cost grows linearly rather than quadratically with the radius, use a Minkowski chain with LP weights. It improves on octagons and Bresenham decompositions, but not on the sup-closure: up to radius $50$, the 13-direction periodic chain has error at most $1.04$ pixels, and larger radii need larger libraries (Fig.~\ref{fig:error}b).
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
Opening by each digital disk of a library is not a granulometry, and its size distributions can increase. The classical sup-closure fixes this at no cost and should be used instead. Single-element families cannot do as well: with mean-radius steps $\delta$, their error is $\Theta(\rho/\delta^2)$, and bounded steps force linear growth in every dimension. Open questions: the optimal rate when only the fitted radius has bounded steps, between $\rho^{1/3}$ and $\rho^{1/2}$; whether single-element granulometries that are not Minkowski chains, built from unions of translates as in~\cite{normand2003}, can escape the bound; and matching upper bounds in $\Z^3$.
